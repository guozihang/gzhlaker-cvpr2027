\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Video copy localization (VCL) returns the corresponding temporal intervals in a query
video and a reference video. Unlike whole-video retrieval, it must identify partial copies
and their boundaries on both axes, despite cropping, overlays, re-encoding or speed
changes~\cite{vcsl2022,transvcl2023}. We study the pairwise task given fixed pretrained
ISC descriptors, rather than searching a video library.

Classical methods use geometric voting or temporal alignment; learned methods detect copy
patterns in similarity maps or enhance frame interactions before
localization~\cite{vcsl2022,spd2021,transvcl2023}. Dense comparison considers $MN$ frame
pairs for sequences of $M$ and $N$ descriptors; chunking reduces peak allocation, but still
requires the comparisons inside the processed chunk pairs. When copies occupy a small part
of the time plane, discovery and boundary refinement need not use the same domain.

FastVCL separates them. Sparse mutual matches support local affine temporal tracks;
dual-axis support checks and gap splitting produce finite tubes, within which full-resolution
features are compared and aligned. Extension moves the search beyond the outer anchors but
does not guarantee that those boundaries are recoverable. Sparse proposals may miss a copy,
and local alignment cannot create evidence outside its search domain.

\paragraph{Two errors that more arithmetic cannot fix.}
The same separation exposes failure modes of the fixed descriptors. Repeated textures,
static logos and similar shots can pass mutual matching and RANSAC, producing pseudo-tracks
that consume refinement and become false intervals. Conversely, a genuine copy can be
fragmented when a weak or edited middle section breaks anchor support, so the core reports
several pieces where one continuous copy exists. Both errors are questions of source
identity, not of alignment quality: additional ISC comparisons on the same descriptors do
not decide whether two regions come from the same underlying footage.

\paragraph{Two budgeted decision points.}
A small number of visual decisions can address these errors, but their placement matters.
Verifying only final candidates cannot remove a pseudo-anchor that already consumed a track,
and cannot rejoin fragments that were emitted separately. We therefore query one shared
model, named the \texttt{seltive model}, at two points: before fitting, where a decisive
verdict may delete a doubtful anchor; and after alignment, where a decisive verdict may merge
two fragments that are plausibly one continuous copy. Each decision is \emph{selective} (a
small declared budget of questions per pair), \emph{conservative} (only a decisive
\texttt{NOT\_COPY} or \texttt{MERGE} acts, and geometry or connection evidence can veto it),
and \emph{reversible} (uncertainty, budget exhaustion or failure leaves the core evidence
unchanged). The model is pretrained and post-trained by its authors on external data; we add
no training on our benchmark, and we do not read its scores as calibrated probabilities.

\paragraph{Hypotheses.}
The core claims remain:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{H1: candidate coverage.} Reducing coarse dot products retains useful candidate
coverage, including on short segments.
\item \textbf{H2: boundary recovery.} On identical candidates, in-tube local alignment improves
endpoints without obtaining the apparent gain solely by dropping difficult segments.
\item \textbf{H3: system benefit.} Less alignment work improves measured quality--cost trade-offs
on the same hardware after proposal, transfer and post-processing costs are included.
\end{itemize}
The two decision points add three falsifiable hypotheses. \textbf{H4-A:} selective anchor
verification reduces pseudo-tracks and false intervals, or downstream work, within a
predeclared recall-loss limit; deleting anchors is not success by itself. \textbf{H4-F:} a
visual relation verdict plus a connection check repairs true over-segmentation and avoids
bridging real non-copy gaps, relative to purely geometric fusion. \textbf{H4-B:} at the same
measured end-to-end cost, the two-stage policy beats random selection, cheap geometric rules
and an unfiltered core. A strong simple sparse-to-band baseline and controlled changes are
necessary to distinguish mechanism gains from larger budgets, different candidates or simply
having seen more pixels.

\paragraph{Scope and current contributions.}
This draft provides (i) a finite-tube specification and cost accounting that separates
quadratic coarse arithmetic from bounded refinement, (ii) two explicitly specified budgeted
decision points with action rules, geometry and connection gates, abstention and fallback,
and a shared budget and cache contract, (iii) a falsifiable experiment matrix that fixes the
correct upstream input for each question, and (iv) an evaluation protocol with explicit
completion, threshold, risk and timing rules. These are methodological artifacts; empirical
benefit and novelty relative to the baseline remain unresolved. ``Training-free'' refers only
to the core: ISC is pretrained and validation labels select parameters. The
\texttt{seltive model} branch (Section~\ref{sec:method-decisions}) adds an external model,
original frames and additional compute, and is reported separately.

\paragraph{Evidence status.}
The code is a preliminary prototype whose continuation rule differs from the DP specified
here and whose visual-verification path is anchor-level only. No frozen benchmark, model call
or GPU timing supports the paper; the main tables retain visible placeholders, and
development/synthetic checks are only in the supplement. ``Fast'' is a project name, not a
speed claim. The supplement contrasts the dense and sparse domains conceptually.
