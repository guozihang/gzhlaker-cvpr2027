\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Video copy localization (VCL) returns the corresponding temporal intervals in a query
video and a reference video. Unlike whole-video retrieval, it must identify partial copies
and their boundaries on both axes, despite cropping, overlays, re-encoding or speed
changes~\cite{vcsl2022,transvcl2023}. We study the pairwise task given fixed pretrained
ISC descriptors, rather than searching a video library; ``Fast'' is a project name, not a
speed claim.

Classical methods use geometric voting or temporal alignment; learned methods detect copy
patterns in similarity maps or enhance frame interactions before
localization~\cite{vcsl2022,spd2021,transvcl2023}. Dense comparison considers $MN$ frame
pairs for sequences of $M$ and $N$ descriptors; chunking reduces peak allocation, but still
requires the comparisons inside the processed chunk pairs. When copies occupy a small part
of the time plane, discovery and boundary refinement need not use the same domain.

FastVCL separates them. Sparse mutual matches support local affine temporal tracks;
dual-axis support checks and gap splitting produce finite tubes, within which full-resolution
features are compared and aligned. Extension moves the search beyond the outer anchors but
does not guarantee that those boundaries are recoverable. Sparse proposals may miss a copy,
and local alignment cannot create evidence outside its search domain.

\paragraph{Source identity is not settled by geometry alone.}
Repeated textures, static logos and similar shots can pass mutual matching and RANSAC,
producing pseudo-tracks that consume refinement and become false intervals. Conversely, a
genuine copy can be fragmented when a weak or edited middle section breaks anchor support, so
the core reports several pieces where one continuous copy exists. Both errors concern source
identity rather than alignment quality. More ISC comparisons, additional context or post-hoc
geometric rules may still help; whether a limited number of visual decisions provides
information beyond those cheap controls is a question we test, not a premise.

\paragraph{Two budgeted interventions.}
A post-hoc verifier cannot undo work already spent on a pseudo-anchor, and it can rejoin
fragments only if it explicitly constructs a relation between them. We therefore query one
shared model, named the \texttt{seltive model}, at two intervention points: before fitting,
where a decisive verdict may delete a doubtful anchor; and after alignment, where a decisive
verdict may merge two fragments that are plausibly one continuous copy. Each decision is
\emph{selective} (a small declared budget of questions per pair), \emph{conservative} (only
a decisive \texttt{NOT\_COPY} or \texttt{MERGE} acts, and geometry or connection evidence can
veto it), and \emph{abstaining} (uncertainty, budget exhaustion or failure leaves the
affected item at its core state). Abstention is per item, not a global inverse: in
\textsc{on} mode, one failed action does not guarantee that the final output equals the core
output, because other accepted actions may still change it. Only \textsc{off} and
\textsc{shadow} modes must reproduce the core output item by item. The model is pretrained
and post-trained by its authors on external data; we add no training on our benchmark, and
we do not read its scores as calibrated probabilities.

\paragraph{Research questions.}
We organize the study around three questions rather than six independent hypotheses.
\textbf{RQ1 (core):} does sparse-to-tube retain useful candidate coverage and improve
boundary recovery over a simple sparse-to-band pipeline at equal executed coarse work, and
does any gain survive end-to-end cost? This covers H1--H3 in Section~\ref{sec:exp}.
\textbf{RQ2 (anchor filtering):} does a budgeted visual filter reduce pseudo-tracks or false
intervals, within a predeclared recall-loss limit, relative to no action, random selection and
cheap geometric rules (H4-A)? Deleting anchors is not success by itself. \textbf{RQ3 (fusion
and allocation):} does a visual relation verdict plus a connection check repair true
over-segmentation and avoid bridging real non-copy gaps, relative to purely geometric fusion
(H4-F); and at the same measured cost, does the two-stage allocation beat a single stage or
random selection (H4-B)? A strong sparse-to-band baseline and controlled changes are
necessary to distinguish mechanism gains from larger budgets, different candidates or simply
having seen more pixels.

\paragraph{Contributions and scope.}
This draft makes three contributions, each conditional on evidence that is still pending.
(i)~\emph{Method:} a sparse-to-tube localization framework that spends a limited visual
decision budget at two intervention points, with one shared model, budget and fallback
contract; mutual matching, RANSAC and local dynamic programming are classical components and
are not claimed as original. (ii)~\emph{Mechanism:} selection-only filtering, abstention and
geometric/connection constraints, with stage-specific controls that isolate how each action
changes pseudo-tracks, fragmented copies and wrong-bridge risk. (iii)~\emph{Protocol:}
fixed-upstream-input comparisons, explicit action-risk denominators and a quality--cost
analysis. The experiment matrix, cache fields and audit records are reproduction material,
not separate contributions.
